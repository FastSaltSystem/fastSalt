## Electricity demand and nuclear materials

### Slide 1. Fast Salt System and EI assisted engineering

**0–1 min elapsed (1 min)** · Introduce how EI-assisted reactor design can engage MSU faculty and students.

I’m here to talk about how a nuclear reactor can fit into energy needs and nuclear waste disposal needs. And how the design work can give students and faculty in different disciplines real projects to work on while using Electronic Intelligence.

### Slide 2. EI (Electronic Intelligence) power demand

**1–3 min elapsed (2 min)** · Compare the projected increase in US data-center electricity use with the annual output of 41 additional WISTRs.

The bars show annual electricity use by all US data centers. At 600 MWe and a 95% capacity factor, one WISTR would produce just under 5 TWh in a year. That additional need could be met by 41 additional WISTR reactors.

Why might actual demand depart from that projection? Less energy per task pulls it down. More users and longer, more complex tasks pull it up. The tenfold improvement refers to a recent trend for comparable tasks.

### Slide 3. Solar nearly does it

**3–5 min elapsed (2 min)** · Separate the annual energy comparison from delivery at the time and place of use.

Annual US data-center consumption grows by about 40 TWh each year on average. At the recent installation rate, one year’s 46.6 GW of new solar panels, rated in DC, could deliver about 74 TWh in a full year at the assumed 18.2% capacity factor.

That leaves two practical questions. Where is the power needed, and at what hour? Storage and transmission each address part of that problem. An operator facing negative wholesale prices could benefit from reducing reactor output during a surplus. The other reason I’m pursuing this system is what it could do with spent fuel.

## Neutron spectra, spent fuel and the fuel cycle

### Slide 5. Thermal and fast neutron reactors

**7–9 min elapsed (2 min)** · Explain why neutron energy affects what the reactor can do with its fuel.

Think of “thermal” and “fast” as descriptions of the neutrons that sustain fission. In a familiar light-water reactor, collisions with the water slow neutrons down. The choice of coolant and other core materials in a fast reactor allows much more of the neutron population to retain high energy.

Neutron capture can form heavier nuclei. Fission consumes them and releases energy. The competition between those reactions helps explain why both the neutron spectrum and the choice of fuel matter for our waste-management goal.

### Slide 6. What remains in spent fuel

**9–10.5 min elapsed (1.5 min)** · Read spent fuel as several material groups with different destinations.

Uranium accounts for most of the remaining heavy metal, and most of that uranium is U-238. The plutonium and higher actinides are particularly interesting to us because they can serve as fuel. Fission products contribute decay heat, and the cladding is another material to handle.

### Slide 7. Recycling and net actinide consumption

**10.5–12 min elapsed (1.5 min)** · Explain what makes WISTR a burner and why heat-producing wastes matter.

Recycling returns actinides to the fuel cycle. Subsequent fission is what consumes them. A fast reactor can be designed to produce more fissile material than it consumes or to consume it on net. The latter is the purpose of WISTR.

There is a second waste-management question. Separating heat-producing cesium and strontium could ease the thermal demands on disposal of the remaining material. Disposal requirements still depend on the composition of each resulting stream.

### Slide 8. The Fast Salt System fuel cycle

**12–14 min elapsed (2 min)** · Trace the complete fuel cycle before looking at its equipment.

Spent Nuclear Fuel is produced by the Light Water Reactor. We have a process that can convert this oxide fuel (a ceramic) to a chloride salt. This is then processed in a Refiner that removes many of the fission products. This is then suitable as fuel for the WISTR reactor. Depleted uranium metal is produced by the converter and intermediate level waste and hot waste by the refiner. This separation of waste types allows convenient disposal.

## Salt systems, core sectors and fueling research

### Slide 9. Converting spent fuel into crude fuel salt

**14–16 min elapsed (2 min)** · Identify the converter’s input, output and chemistry questions.

The proposed converter receives solid spent oxide fuel, salt and carbon and produces a crude chloride salt for the refiner. The method shown combines oxide reduction, chlorination and removal of uranium from the salt.

### Slide 10. Refining and recycling fuel salt

**16–18 min elapsed (2 min)** · Explain why the refiner receives fuel from two sources.

The Refiner takes molten salt with fission products and returns a cleaner fuel for the WISTR.

Actinides such as uranium, plutonium are preferentially reduced and dissolved in the liquid-metal cathode. This liquid metal is then pumped to the chlorinator where it recombines with the chlorine.

### Slide 11. Separate fuel salt and coolant salt

**18–20 min elapsed (2 min)** · Distinguish the fuel inventory from the circulating coolant.

The fuel salt is nominally 55% salt, 25% depleted uranium chloride, and 20% transuranium chlorides. The coolant salt circulates through tubes in the tanks, picks up heat and carries it to the steam generators.

### Slide 12. Chloride coolant versus sodium

**20–22 min elapsed (2 min)** · Explain why the project is studying chloride coolant alongside the sodium precedent.

The choice of coolant is very important. Sodium conducts heat well, allows smaller cores and has some fast-reactor operating experience. But sodium reacts strongly with air and water and requires extensive precautions. Chloride salt brings its own questions about purity, corrosion and freezing temperature, but allows simpler designs.

### Slide 13. The six-sector core

**22–23.5 min elapsed (1.5 min)** · Distinguish the six removable sectors from the six fuel regions in each.

The active core is 3.8 m in diameter and 3.4 m high. The cylindrical core is divided into six pie-shaped sectors. Within each sector there are six fuel regions. Dividing the core into sectors allows convenient transportation and maintenance. Dividing each sector into regions allows moving fuel from outer regions toward the center over time to reduce the power peak and higher power operation.

## Cooling, absorber, fuel storage and maintenance

### Slide 15. Coolant circulation and heat removal

**25–27 min elapsed (2 min)** · Follow the coolant from the cold inlet back to the steam generators.

Cooler salt enters from the side and descends through the downcomer around the core. It turns inward at the bottom, rises through the reflector and core passages, and collects in the hot plenum. From there it flows to the steam generator. The plant has three such loops. The steam generators also serve as emergency air coolers as well. Because steam is a vapor, it takes a lot of surface area to transfer heat to steam and steam generators consequently are very large. They easily dwarf everything else, including the reactor.

### Slide 16. The dual-tube steam-generator concept

**27–29 min elapsed (2 min)** · Show how counterflow and the separate air path fit in one generator.

The water and steam are at very high pressures (supercritical at 24 Mpa). The coolant salt is at very low pressures and passes through the reactor. To provide separation without the complication of an intermediate loop, tubes are provided for both salt and steam, so that steam leaks can pass to the air without pressurizing the reactor. Air passages through the steam generator also serve as emergency air cooler.

The steam generator design is the subject of a MSU capstone design project.

### Slide 17. Coolant P&ID

**29–31 min elapsed (2 min)** · Use the P&ID to trace one of the three coolant loops.

This is a piping and instrumentation diagram. This shows the process, pumps, and instruments in the cooling design. The pump is on the cold leg. The next two rows repeat that arrangement.

The drawing records what connects to what. Later we’ll use the 3D model to see the length and bends of the physical pipe route.

### Slide 18. Liquid absorber

**31–32.5 min elapsed (1.5 min)** · Locate the absorber tank, reservoir and gas module in the drawing.

Moving neutron-absorbing salt into the tank gives the reactor a means of shutdown. Each radial assembly has primary and secondary chambers, each served by its own module.

The different colors and vessel shapes help identify what is being moved.

### Slide 26. Wall Temperature Evolution in Station Black-Out

**43.5–46 min elapsed (2.5 min)** · Read Michigan’s protected station-blackout temperature history.

In the case Michigan analyzed, a station blackout triggers shutdown and the pumps coast down. The modeled wall temperature drops to a minimum after about 2.7 minutes. It then rises as decay heat continues. The later peak occurs after about 10.1 hours. After that, heat removal exceeds the declining decay heat and the temperature falls. Michigan’s analysis reported no freezing of fuel or coolant in this case. Heat removal is through the Steam Generator Auxiliary Coolers (SGACS) and the Reactor Vessel Auxiliary Cooler (RVAC). Both remove heat via air cooling.

Credit Hangyu Lee and Won Sik Yang for the calculation and figure.

## EI collaboration, engineering education and discussion

### Slide 28. Electronic Intelligence help is essential now

**48–49 min elapsed (1 min)** · Use the bulldozer analogy to explain how students can develop engineering judgment while using EI.

A bulldozer changes how much a small team can do. The operator still needs to know what grade to cut, how to run the machine, and how to recognize when the work is going wrong.

That’s what interests me about EI in engineering. Students can take on a substantial problem and learn the fundamentals they need to judge what the tool gives them.

### Slide 29. Tools for EI collaboration

**49–50.5 min elapsed (1.5 min)** · Show how the project’s tools support one design conversation.

These are the tools that allow effectively working with an EI. EIs are excellent at developing programs. So turn your project into a programming problem. These are the free tools that make an effective work environment. Visual Studio Code for code editing, documentation and viewing changes. Markdown is text based allowing versions of documentation. Hurmet combines documentation and calculations while allowing versioning. Python is used for more complicated calculations and builds the geometry that can be inspected by rendering it in Blender. GitHub records revisions and gives collaborators a common place to review them.

EI helps me move from a question or directions to a calculation or drawing; it can find references and do calculations.. The files give us something specific to check and revise together.

### Slide 30. EI collaboration on coolant pump sizing

**50.5–53.5 min elapsed (3 min)** · With revised flow, generator geometry and itemized losses, the estimated electrical input for three pumps fell from 5.17 MW to 2.50 MW.

I first allowed 50% above the steam-generator friction loss for everything else in the loop. The 3D modeled lengths and bends went into the Hurmet worksheet along with the core and generator losses.

The original worksheet gave 5.17 MW for all three pumps at 22,699 kg/s total coolant flow. The revised case uses 18,500 kg/s and the newer generator geometry. Reapplying the old allowance to that revised case gives the separate 1.54 MW comparison below.

I supplied the newer generator workbook. At the same flow and geometry, the two methods give 1.54 MW and 2.50 MW of electrical input for all three pumps. For an oral check, 3.72 m³/s × 172 kPa is about 640 kW of hydraulic power per pump. Allowing for the pump and motor efficiencies gives about 835 kW electrical per pump.
